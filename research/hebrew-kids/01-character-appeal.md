# 01 — Character Appeal Mechanics (Global Research)

Why kids 2–8 can't look away: decoded from Stitch, Grogu, Minions, Bluey.
**Purpose: extract the *appeal recipe* for an ORIGINAL character — never copy Disney IP.**

## (a) The 8 actionable design traits

### 1. Kindchenschema ("baby schema") proportions — non-negotiable
Konrad Lorenz's 1949 feature set: **large head relative to body, high protruding forehead, large
low-set eyes, chubby cheeks, small nose and mouth, plump body, short thick limbs.** Experimentally
proven: Glocker et al. (2009, PMC3260535, N=122) — photos manipulated toward high baby schema rated
significantly cuter (F(2,59)=137.3, p<0.001), triggered stronger caretaking motivation and greater
**nucleus accumbens** activation (reward center). Borgi et al. (2014): 3–6-year-olds already
prefer high-infantile faces across species — children lock onto baby-schema eyes before they can read.
Stephen Jay Gould documented Disney exploiting this: Mickey Mouse redrawn over decades toward
ever-larger head/eyes, "aging in reverse" toward an infant. Grogu is the modern endpoint.

**Spec:** head ≈ ⅓–½ of total height; eyes large, wide-set, low on the face; forehead high; body
soft/round/plump; limbs short and stubby; no sharp angles anywhere on the silhouette.

### 2. "Cute, but a little weird" — the tension that prevents blandness
Favreau on Grogu's winning concept art: *"It looked cute, but it also looked a little weird… It
can't just be cute."* Pure cute is forgettable; cute + one odd trait (Stitch's extra arms/antennae,
Grogu's wrinkles/giant ears, Minions' pill-bodies/goggles) creates memorability + merch distinctiveness.

**Spec:** pair the baby-schema face with exactly ONE exaggerated, slightly strange signature feature.

### 3. Mischievous-but-kind, chaotic-but-lovable personality
Stitch was engineered "to cause chaos and destruction," then rehabilitated by love. Minions are
banana-obsessed slapstick agents whose hearts are loyal. This is the toddler's own inner life
reflected back: high impulse, poor regulation, good intentions, constant minor catastrophe.
Kids don't want a well-behaved character — they want a character whose chaos is forgiven.

**Spec:** impulse-driven, mildly destructive playfulness that always resolves into warmth; the
character errs like a child and is loved anyway.

### 4. Vulnerability + smallness in a big world (protective instinct)
Grogu is 50 but reads as a helpless toddler; Stitch is a feared superweapon who wants a family;
Minion Bob clutches a teddy bear, "easily frightened." Baby schema triggers caretaking; explicit
vulnerability (smallness, fear, need for comfort) doubles it.

**Spec:** the character should periodically be scared, sad, lost, or in need of comfort — giving
the child viewer the caretaker role.

### 5. Pre-verbal / gibberish voice that works in every language
Minionese was engineered for this: Pierre Coffin built it from Spanish/Italian/Indonesian/Japanese,
choosing words "not by the meaning, but rather the melody." Audiences can't parse words but "you
absolutely feel what they're saying." Silent-film logic (Chaplin/Keaton): emotion carried by prosody,
not vocabulary — exactly how a 2-year-old processes speech. Grogu's voice = bat-eared fox + kinkajou
+ **real infant vocals**, pitched up.

**Spec:** communicate via coos, giggles, gasps, squeals, melody-first gibberish rather than dialogue.
For Hebrew: simple Hebrew + expressive prosody.

### 6. Found family / belonging as the emotional payload
Lilo & Stitch's engine is *ʻohana* — "nobody gets left behind or forgotten." Grogu is a foundling
adopted by a bounty hunter. The "I am loved and I belong" message makes attachment stick beyond
surface cute. Commercially massive: Lilo & Stitch = top-10 Disney franchise, **$2.6B retail by 2024,
+$4B FY2025 (~$7.9B total)** — the theme sells across 20+ years and two generations.

**Spec:** build the micro-narrative around the character being accepted into a group/family; end on
belonging, not just a gag.

### 7. Distinct, repeated sound signatures + catchphrases
Minions: "Banana!", "Bello!", Bob's gasps. Stitch: "Ohana means family." Grogu: the coo + waddle +
reach-for-buttons gesture. Replayable, imitable, meme-able — toddlers reproduce them, deepening
attachment. Personality through vocal mannerism (Kevin admonishes=adult, Stuart "meh"=teen, Bob gasps=baby).

**Spec:** 2–3 signature non-word sounds + one catchphrase repeated every short, + a signature gesture.
For Hebrew: a short, imitable Hebrew catchphrase.

### 8. A recognizable, saturated color identity
Minions = yellow, Stitch = blue, Grogu = green, Bluey = blue heelers against vibrant pastels. One
dominant high-saturation body color is both a design asset and a developmental legibility aid —
infants see high-contrast saturated colors best.

## (b) Format patterns that hook ages 2–8 — with specifics

### Repetition is the core engine (ages 2–5 especially)
BabyCenter expert answer: toddlers demand the same video endlessly because repetition is HOW they
learn — limited long-term memory, repetition moves material to durable memory, predictability provides
emotional security. CoComelon experts (Romper): psychologist Nicole Beurkens — "Children's brains
thrive on patterns and routine"; therapist Laura Froyen — repetition "is how they learn best."

**Spec:** repeat the hook/phrase/gag a minimum of 3× per short; make shorts rewatch-identical
(loop-ability serves the rewatch drive); keep series structure rigidly formulaic so the child can
predict and "participate."

### Call-and-response with engineered pauses (the Ms. Rachel method)
Ms. Rachel (20.2M subs, 16.2B views as of June 2026; Netflix's most-viewed children's season H1 2025)
built her format on speech-therapist techniques. Documented set (Child Mind Institute, SLP Rachel
Cortese): **pause and wait** mid-song so the child fills the final word; **imitate** the child's
babble; **interpret** gestures into words; **expand/recast** (child: "red truck" → "Yes, a big red
truck"); **narrate like a sportscaster**; respond immediately; model only "the next level of complexity."

**Spec:** character addresses viewer directly; ask a question, hold a genuine 2–4s expectant pause,
then answer warmly; narrate actions as they happen.

### Pacing: fast enough to hold, slow enough to not harm
CoComelon experts: "constant action, but not so fast-paced that it is overwhelming."
**The caution data point (Lillard & Peterson, PMC9923845):** 60 four-year-olds, 9 minutes of viewing.
Fast-paced cartoon (scene change **every ~11s**) significantly impaired executive function vs.
educational show (change every ~34s) and drawing: delay-of-gratification wait 146s vs 257s/242s.

**Spec for shorts:** visible action/reaction beats every 2–4s, but keep the *scene* stable (no full
scene cuts faster than ~10s); movement within frame, not jump-cut chaos. For a ≤60s short: one
setting, one continuous event. **Calm pacing is our differentiator** (see market gap).

### Color & visual legibility
Froyen: babies' maturing eyesight → "prefer brighter colors with more contrast." SLP Jocelyn Wood:
red/blue/green dominate; distinct saturated hues "stimulate a child's cognition."

**Spec:** high-saturation primaries, strong figure-ground contrast, uncluttered backgrounds, one
focal character per shot.

### Music & songs
Nursery-rhyme/song videos are the single most-dominant format in children's YouTube — the all-time
most-viewed list is ~⅓ kids' songs: Baby Shark 17.24B (first video ever to 10B), Wheels on the Bus
9.28B, Bath Song 7.5B, Johny Johny Yes Papa 7.2B, Phonics Song 6.99B. Winning songs = simple melody
+ relentless repetition + on-screen action synced to lyrics ("doo doo doo" = pure prosody hook).

**Spec:** every short should be singable; lyric = one repeated 2–6-word phrase; action exactly synced
to musical beats; tempo upbeat but speech-paced (singable by a 4-year-old).

### Format menu by age band
- **2–3:** songs/rhymes, call-and-response, character-to-camera, colors/numbers/words, cause-effect gags.
- **4–5:** + simple moral/mini-story (problem → chaos → warm resolution), dance-along/imitation, "spot the thing."
- **6–8:** + character personality humor, challenge/game formats, pretend-play skits (Vlad & Niki
  engine: 150.7M subs, 122.7B views, 21 channels × 18 languages — pretend-play + localization = global scale).

### Dual-audience: child now, parent as gatekeeper
Bluey (most-streamed US show of 2024 AND 2025 per Nielsen — 55.6B minutes 2024, ~45B 2025) wins
because parents co-view willingly. For shorts: the parent doesn't need to laugh — but the short must
be *non-irritating* and feel safe/warm, because parents curate the under-8 feed. **The Feb 2026 NYT
investigation into AI-generated kids' video "slop" is actively pushing parents toward trusted
character brands. A consistent, warm original character is the antidote and the opportunity.**

## (c) What the biggest kids' shorts/videos share

1. **A locked, recurring character front-and-center** — JJ (CoComelon 160M+ subs), Pinkfong's Baby
   Shark, Masha (4.67B views on one episode), Vlad & Niki. One face, one color, one personality, never redesigned.
2. **Music or a signature sound in the first seconds** — audio is the hook; toddlers can't read titles.
3. **Hook inside ~2 seconds via character face or motion** — big baby-schema face fills frame immediately,
   already moving/reacting. No intros, no logos, no establishing shots.
4. **One idea per video, resolved** — one song, one color, one gag, one mini-problem. Never two.
5. **Predictable structure, endless rewatchability** — formula identical across episodes; loops or ends
   where it began. A single nursery rhyme at 9.28B views = massive rewatching.
6. **Participation built in** — sing along, answer the question in the pause, copy the dance, find the object.
7. **Global-by-construction** — gibberish/simple lyrics, visual storytelling, then aggressive localization
   (Vlad & Niki: 21 channels/18 languages; Bluey: 60 countries, dubbed in 20).
8. **Warm emotional resolution** — chaos without warmth reads as scary to a 3-year-old; warmth without
   chaos reads as boring.
9. **Parent-safe** — no startling cuts, no mean humor, no uncanny AI weirdness.
